\title{\bf When Does a Variational Quantum Classifier Head Help?\\
\large An Empirical Comparison Against Capacity-Matched Classical Heads\\
on MNIST and CIFAR-10}

\author{%

\begin{abstract}
Hybrid quantum--classical models, in which a classical neural network feeds a
variational quantum circuit (VQC), are a leading candidate architecture for
near-term quantum machine learning. Yet controlled comparisons against
classical baselines of comparable parameter count remain scarce, and results
on datasets beyond MNIST are rarer still. We train a shared convolutional
backbone with three interchangeable classifier heads, namely a linear layer, a
near-parameter-matched multilayer perceptron (MLP), and an 8-qubit VQC, on
stratified low-data subsets of MNIST and CIFAR-10, with three random seeds per
configuration. On MNIST, the VQC head outperforms the near-matched MLP at
every training-set size, by roughly 7--18 percentage points, and trails a
smaller linear reference head by only 6--8 points. On CIFAR-10 the ordering
inverts: the VQC falls behind both classical heads at every training-set size;
the deficit narrows as data is added but remains large. Notably, the VQC never
outperforms the simple linear head in any condition. Data re-uploading
degrades accuracy in every configuration tested, with the strongest
re-uploading schedule driving the circuit into underfitting
(train--validation gap ${<}1$ point). Our results suggest that the dominant
constraint as image complexity grows is not primarily optimization difficulty
but the fixed rotation-angle encoding applied to a narrow bottleneck: the
quantum head is competitive precisely when the bottleneck embedding is already
nearly linearly separable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Variational quantum circuits (VQCs) trained by gradient descent are the
workhorse of near-term quantum machine learning
\citep{benedetti2019parameterized, cerezo2021variational}. In image
classification, the dominant hybrid pattern delegates feature extraction to a
classical convolutional network and classification to a small quantum circuit
\citep{mitarai2018quantum, havlicek2019supervised}. Reported successes,
however, are difficult to interpret: quantum heads are frequently compared
against classical baselines with different parameter budgets, different
backbones, or no controlled seed replication, and evaluation is concentrated
on MNIST, whose features are nearly linearly separable after even shallow
convolutional processing.

This paper asks a narrow question with a controlled experiment: \emph{holding
the backbone, the bottleneck dimension, and the optimizer fixed, and matching
the head parameter count as closely as the architectures allow, when does
replacing a classical classifier head with a VQC help, and when does it
hurt?} We contribute:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled comparison of linear, near-parameter-matched MLP, and VQC
        heads on a shared CNN backbone, across three low-data regimes on both
        MNIST and CIFAR-10. In total we report 90 training runs: 2 datasets
        $\times$ 3 training-set sizes $\times$ 5 head configurations (linear,
        MLP, and quantum with three re-uploading schedules) $\times$ 3 seeds.
  \item Evidence that the VQC head's relative standing depends strongly on
        dataset complexity: it beats the near-matched MLP on MNIST at every
        training-set size, loses to it on CIFAR-10 at every training-set
        size, and never outperforms a smaller linear head on either dataset.
  \item An ablation of data re-uploading \citep{perez2020data} showing that,
        in this bottlenecked hybrid setting, every re-uploading schedule
        tested reduces accuracy, and the most aggressive schedule induces
        underfitting rather than overfitting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Architecture and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Shared backbone.}
All heads consume the same embedding: a CNN backbone ends in global average
pooling followed by a linear projection to a $d=8$ dimensional bottleneck. For
MNIST the backbone has three convolutional blocks (32--64--128 channels,
${\sim}94$K parameters); for CIFAR-10, four blocks (64--128--256--256
channels, ${\sim}0.96$M parameters). The backbone is trained jointly with the
head in every run; gradients flow through the quantum circuit into the
convolutional layers.

\paragraph{Quantum head.}
The bottleneck vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^8$ is encoded into an 8-qubit
circuit by single-qubit rotations $R_y\!\left(\pi \tanh x_i\right)$ on qubit
$i$. The variational block repeats $L=4$ layers, each applying learned
$R_y(\theta)\,R_z(\phi)$ rotations per qubit followed by a ring of CNOT
entanglers. The circuit outputs $\braket{Z_i}$ for each qubit, and a final
classical linear layer maps the 8 expectation values to 10 class logits. The
head totals 154 trainable parameters: $8 \times 2 \times 4 = 64$ rotation
angles plus $8 \times 10 + 10 = 90$ readout weights. Rotation angles are
initialized from $\mathcal{U}(-0.1, 0.1)$, keeping the initial state close to
$\ket{0}^{\otimes 8}$ where gradients are well-conditioned; we found
uncontrolled initialization to be a dominant source of seed variance.
Circuits are simulated with PennyLane's \texttt{lightning.qubit} statevector
backend using adjoint differentiation \citep{bergholm2018pennylane}.

\paragraph{Data re-uploading.}
We test three encoding schedules. \textsc{none} encodes $\mathbf{x}$ once,
before the first variational layer. \textsc{light} additionally re-encodes a
rescaled copy ($0.5\mathbf{x}$) midway through the variational block.
\textsc{strong} places one full encoding of $\mathbf{x}$ immediately before
\emph{each} of the $L=4$ variational layers (the first of these serves as the
initial encoding; there is no separate extra encoding), following
P\'{e}rez-Salinas et al.~\citep{perez2020data}.

\paragraph{Classical baselines.}
The \emph{linear} head is a single dense layer (90 parameters). The \emph{MLP}
head is $8 \to 8 \to 10$: one hidden layer of width 8 with ReLU activation, no
dropout or normalization, PyTorch-default (Kaiming-uniform) initialization,
and the same optimizer and learning rate as every other head (not tuned
separately). Its 162 total parameters (weights and biases of both layers) are
the closest the architecture comes to the quantum head's 154; we therefore
describe the MLP as \emph{near}-parameter-matched rather than equal. The
linear head is smaller than both and is included as the strongest simple
reference baseline (a linear probe of the embedding), not as a
capacity-matched comparison.

\paragraph{Training protocol.}
All runs use AdamW (head learning rate $10^{-3}$, backbone $5\times10^{-4}$,
weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine-annealed to zero over the run, batch size 32,
and gradient clipping. Training subsets are stratified samples of the full
training set, preserving class balance. MNIST runs train for 25 epochs with
clip norm 1.0; CIFAR-10 runs for 80 epochs with clip norm 2.0 and standard
augmentation (random crop, horizontal flip, rotation, color jitter). Every
configuration is repeated with seeds $\{0, 1, 2\}$; we report the mean and
\emph{sample} standard deviation over the three seeds. Per-component gradient
norms (backbone vs.\ head) are logged every epoch to monitor trainability.

\paragraph{Evaluation caveat.}
Accuracy is measured on the official 10{,}000-image test split after every
epoch, and we report the best epoch per run. Because no separate validation
split was held out of the training data, the test split participates in
checkpoint selection; we therefore call the metric \emph{test-split accuracy}
rather than test accuracy, and we quantify the effect directly: mean
final-epoch accuracy is within 1.3 points of best-epoch accuracy in every one
of the 30 configurations (within 0.5 points in 26 of them), and no ordering
among heads changes if final-epoch accuracy is used instead. A
train/validation/test protocol is planned for a future revision.

\paragraph{MNIST: the quantum head beats its near-matched baseline but not a
linear probe.}
Table~\ref{tab:mnist} shows the VQC head (\textsc{none}) exceeding the
162-parameter MLP by 17.8, 18.0, and 6.9 points at 250, 500, and 1{,}000
training samples respectively, while trailing the 90-parameter linear head by
a roughly constant 6--8 points. The quantum head is thus the better of the two
similarly sized nonlinear heads, but it never beats the simplest baseline. At
1{,}000 samples it is also the most reproducible model in the study (sample
std 0.3 across seeds). In this regime the 8-dimensional embedding learned by
the backbone is nearly linearly separable (the linear head reaches 89.6\%),
and the VQC's fixed angular encoding $R_y(\pi\tanh x_i)$ preserves enough of
that structure to classify well.

\paragraph{CIFAR-10: the ordering inverts.}
On CIFAR-10 (Table~\ref{tab:cifar}) the same quantum head loses to the same
near-matched MLP at every training-set size, by 14.1, 13.8, and 8.3 points.
Against the linear head the deficit is 20.4 points at 1{,}000 samples and 12.3
points at 4{,}000: the quantum head narrows both gaps as data is added but
remains far behind, whereas on MNIST it was within 8 points of the linear head
everywhere. Training dynamics rule out simple non-convergence: test-split
accuracy moved by less than one point over the final ten epochs in all
CIFAR-10 configurations.

\paragraph{Generalization-gap analysis separates the failure modes.}
At 4{,}000 CIFAR-10 samples the final-epoch train--test gaps are 10.3
(linear), 6.9 (MLP), 5.5 (quantum \textsc{none}), and 0.8 points (quantum
\textsc{strong}). The classical heads overfit; the plain quantum head fits
moderately; the strong re-uploading circuit \emph{underfits}: its training
accuracy barely exceeds its test-split accuracy at 35\%. The VQC is not
memorizing and failing to generalize; it lacks the capacity, under this
encoding, to fit CIFAR-10's bottleneck features at all. This is consistent
with viewing the fixed encoding as a severe architectural prior: a Fourier
feature map of bounded frequency content \citep{schuld2021effect} that MNIST
embeddings happen to satisfy and CIFAR-10 embeddings do not.

\paragraph{The encoding saturates on CIFAR-10.}
Direct inspection of trained checkpoints supports the encoding-constraint
account. Passing the test split through the backbone of a trained
4{,}000-sample CIFAR-10 quantum run, 16.7\% of bottleneck activations fall in
the saturated region of the encoding ($|\tanh z| > 0.9$) and 5.0\% are
essentially pinned ($|\tanh z| > 0.99$); the corresponding MNIST run yields
4.3\% and 0.0\%. Saturated dimensions receive vanishing gradient through the
encoding and collapse distinct feature values onto nearly identical rotation
angles, so the effective resolution of the quantum head degrades exactly on
the dataset where more resolution is needed.

\paragraph{Data re-uploading hurts monotonically.}
Across all six dataset/size conditions, accuracy orders strictly as
\textsc{none} $>$ \textsc{light} $>$ \textsc{strong}. On CIFAR-10 at 2{,}000
samples the strong schedule costs 18.1 points relative to no re-uploading.
Re-encoding the input before every layer repeatedly rotates the state back
toward a data-determined manifold, discarding the entangled representation
built by earlier variational layers; with only 4 layers and 8 qubits the
circuit apparently cannot compensate. We note this contrasts with the
single-qubit and low-dimensional settings where re-uploading provably adds
expressivity \citep{perez2020data}: expressivity gains at the function-class
level do not automatically survive joint training through a CNN backbone.

\paragraph{Seed variance as a trainability signal.}
Classical heads are nearly deterministic across seeds on CIFAR-10 (sample std
$\leq 0.9$ throughout), while quantum heads vary by up to 7.8 points. With
initialization already controlled (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), the residual
variance indicates a rugged optimization landscape with multiple basins,
which is mild evidence of the trainability pathologies predicted for VQCs
\citep{mcclean2018barren}, even at a depth and width far below where barren
plateaus formally set in. Optimization difficulty is therefore present, but it
is not the dominant effect: even the best seed of the best quantum
configuration falls well short of the mean linear-head accuracy on CIFAR-10.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The two datasets bracket the regime where a small VQC head is useful, and the
honest summary is conditional: the VQC can outperform a similarly small MLP on
MNIST, but it never outperforms a simpler linear probe, and on CIFAR-10 it
underperforms both classical heads. When the backbone can compress the task
into a nearly linearly separable 8-dimensional embedding (MNIST), the quantum
head converts its 154 parameters into substantially more accuracy than a
near-matched MLP; the entangling structure acts as a good inductive bias.
When the task saturates the bottleneck (CIFAR-10), the fixed rotation encoding
becomes the binding constraint, and the saturation measurements above show the
mechanism directly: a classical head can reweight and recombine noisy
embedding dimensions arbitrarily, while the VQC commits each dimension to a
rigid geometric role. The practical implication for hybrid design is that the
quantum head's viability is decided upstream, by whether the classical
compression stage can deliver an embedding matched to the encoding, not by
circuit depth or re-uploading schedule.

\paragraph{Limitations and planned experiments.}
All quantum results are noiseless statevector simulations; hardware noise
would likely further reduce quantum-head accuracy. The evaluation protocol
uses the test split for checkpoint selection (Section~\ref{sec:methods}); the
measured effect is small ($\leq 1.3$ points) but a proper
train/validation/test split is the correct fix. The backbone is trained
jointly with each head, so head comparisons include any differences in the
embedding each head induces; a frozen-backbone experiment, training all heads
on identical fixed embeddings, is the highest-priority follow-up. Two further
experiments would sharpen the encoding-constraint claim: a classical
Fourier-feature baseline (embedding $\to$ $\sin/\cos$ features $\to$ linear
readout) to test whether the VQC's behavior is explained by its encoding
rather than anything quantum-specific, and encoding ablations (learned scale
before $\tanh$, clipped angles, normalized embeddings) to test whether
relieving saturation closes the CIFAR-10 gap. We fixed the bottleneck at
$d=8$ qubits and depth at $L=4$; scaling either is natural but simulation cost
grows quickly. Finally, three seeds per cell bound, but do not precisely
estimate, the seed variance; per-seed results are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:curves}.

\section{Conclusion}

In a controlled comparison spanning 90 training runs, an 8-qubit VQC
classifier head outperforms a near-parameter-matched MLP on MNIST but loses
to it on CIFAR-10, and it never outperforms a smaller linear probe on either
dataset. The CIFAR-10 deficit is driven by underfitting linked to saturation
of the fixed rotation encoding, rather than by overfitting or simple
non-convergence, and data re-uploading reduced accuracy in every
configuration tested. These results argue that empirical claims of quantum
advantage in hybrid classifiers should be qualified by dataset complexity and
accompanied by parameter-matched \emph{and} simple linear baselines. They also
locate the frontier for small quantum heads at the quality of the classical
embedding they are asked to read.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Code, sweep configurations, and per-epoch metrics (including per-component
gradient norms) for all 90 runs are available at
\url{https://github.com/Arkaeyuh/quantcnn}. Experiments used Python 3.14,
PyTorch 2.11, and PennyLane 0.44 (\texttt{lightning.qubit}, adjoint
differentiation) on an Apple M3 Max; quantum circuits were simulated on CPU
with classical components on the MPS backend. Seeds $\{0, 1, 2\}$ control
Python, NumPy, and PyTorch RNGs as well as subset selection.
