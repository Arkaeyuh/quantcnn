\section{What the VQC Head Does to the Backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}

\subsection{The default VQC head lowers bottleneck probe accuracy}
\label{sec:gap}

On CIFAR-10 with 4,000 images, the common probe reaches $69.8\pm1.0$ on backbones trained
with the default VQC head and $74.5\pm0.5$ on backbones trained with a linear head, a paired
difference of $-4.7\pm0.9$ points that is negative for all five seeds. The probe reads the
VQC-trained bottleneck better than the VQC head itself does ($67.8\pm1.3$ test): replacing
the VQC head by the probe raises accuracy by about 2.0 points, and a 4.7-point gap remains
between the two backbones under the common probe. The gap is larger with less data (CIFAR-10:
$-11.2\pm4.0$ and $-11.1\pm1.5$ at 1,000 and 2,000 images) and smaller on MNIST ($-7.9\pm4.2$,
$-2.2\pm0.8$ and $-1.0\pm0.5$ at 250, 500 and 1,000; 3 seeds each). A narrow classical head
lowers probe accuracy as well: MLP-8 backbones probe $3.6\pm0.6$ points below linear-head
backbones on CIFAR-10/4,000, $1.0\pm0.9$ above the VQC. The gap is not an artifact of linear
decoding: a two-layer MLP probe on the same bottlenecks recovers at most 0.4 points more than
the linear probe for any head, and puts the default VQC $4.8\pm0.9$ points below the linear
head (\cref{app:mechanism}). The bottleneck's class information is linearly accessible, and
the VQC-trained one carries less of it. The rest of this section shows that the VQC's share
of the gap depends on its CNOT structure, which no classical head has.

\subsection{Removing the CNOT rings recovers most of the gap}
\label{sec:ring}

Deleting every CNOT and keeping the rest of the head unchanged raises probe accuracy to
$73.6\pm0.5$ and test accuracy to $73.1\pm0.5$ (5 seeds). Against the default VQC this is
$+3.8\pm1.2$ probe and $+5.2\pm1.4$ test points, positive for every seed, and the ring-free
head now probes $2.8\pm0.6$ points above MLP-8. It leaves $0.8\pm0.5$ probe and $1.4\pm0.5$
test points to the linear head. MNIST shows the same direction: ring removal gains
$2.8\pm0.4$ and $1.4\pm0.4$ probe points at 500 and 1,000 images, and the ring-free head's
test accuracy (94.5, 96.4) equals the linear head's (94.3, 96.4) to within 0.2 points.

\subsection{Product order tracks the gap}
\label{sec:order}

Which property of the rings matters? \Cref{fig:hero}b places every head at the product order
of \cref{eq:identity}. Across these eleven heads at four orders, the mean probe accuracy of
every head is below that of every head of lower order. A ring after the last layer only keeps the default's parameter count and
number of rotation layers but raises the order to 5.25; it lowers probe accuracy by
$9.7\pm2.2$ points relative to the default (test $-12.5\pm2.3$; 5 seeds), and the 1- and
2-layer circuits at the same order probe at 56.8 and 61.2 (3 seeds). Across these
circuits, depth and parameter count do not order the cost; product order does.

Two interventions test the products directly. First, classical heads trained end to end on a
circuit's zero-rotation products, with no circuit, land at the circuit's order on the trend:
the products of the default circuit give $68.8\pm1.6$ probe and $67.1\pm1.3$ test, paired
differences to the default VQC of $-1.0\pm2.2$ and $-0.7\pm1.7$ with mixed signs across
seeds. Second, the
\emph{pairs} circuit keeps a CNOT after every layer but has order 1, because its pair CNOTs
cancel at zero rotations. It recovers $3.1\pm1.2$ probe and $4.3\pm1.2$ test points over the
default, positive for every seed, and sits $0.7\pm1.0$ probe points below the ring-free
circuit. Pairs after the
last layer only (order 1.5) probe at $71.2\pm0.7$, between orders 1 and 2.5, as does the
classical head on their products ($71.2\pm1.3$).

In CNOT-derived supports, order rises together with how many inputs different readouts share,
so we also trained classical product heads on designed supports that separate the two
(\cref{tab:support}). Overlap, the mean number of inputs two readouts share, is equal for a
``star'' layout of order 3 and two order-4 layouts, and nearly equal for a star of order 2 and
a window of three consecutive inputs (0.82 vs 0.86). In each of these comparisons the
lower-order head probes higher: by $5.4\pm2.7$ points against an order-4 layout whose support
matrix, like every CNOT-derived one, has full rank, $5.6\pm2.2$ against a rank-5 window, and
$3.9\pm1.9$ for the order-2 star against the order-3 window (5 seeds, all positive). Order
therefore matters at fixed overlap and full rank. At equal order, the stars, which put most
inputs in a single product, probe $3.0\pm0.2$ (3 seeds) and $1.8\pm2.8$ (5 seeds, mixed signs)
points above the windows; the
first of these also differs in rank, so how the coupling is distributed across inputs matters
in addition to order, by an amount these controls do not pin down. Order alone therefore does
not set the cost: an order-3 star probes above the default ring's order-2.5 products (68.9 vs
67.8), and the designed heads also differ in how well they fit the training set (clean training
accuracy 84.8\% for the order-3 star vs 73.6\% for the full-rank order-4 layout, seeds 0--2), so
their contrasts mix structure with optimization.

The pattern also replicates on MNIST with 1,000 images, where we fixed the layouts and wrote
down the predicted ordering before running them. Probe accuracy is 96.5 without CNOTs, 95.5
for pairs after the last layer, 95.1 for the default circuit and 91.5 for a ring after the
last layer; the small pairs-last advantage over the default is $+0.4\pm0.5$ (5 seeds, 4
positive), and the classical head on the default products probes $0.1\pm0.5$ below the default
VQC, and the one on the last-ring products falls below that circuit (90.4).

The identity is exact only at zero rotations, so we check how far the trained circuits move
from it (\cref{app:zeroangle}), fitting an affine map from each circuit's zero-rotation
readouts to its trained readouts on half of the validation set and evaluating it on the other
half. A circuit frozen at its initialization is reproduced almost exactly ($R^2=0.997$). For
the trained default and last-ring circuits the map recovers 87\% and 90\% of the held-out
readout variance, and the backbone's cross-entropy gradient through the remapped products
aligns with the trained one at median cosine 0.86 and 0.87. The products do not carry all
task-relevant detail: the trained readout applied to the remapped products scores 7 and 6
points below the trained circuit, and without the remapping the gradient alignment is weaker
(median 0.59 for the default circuit, and negative for one seed). Low-order circuits move
further ($R^2$ 0.73--0.83); for the ring-free circuit this is the $\sin\enc{j}$ term of
\cref{eq:ringfree}, which its trained rotations use. At order 5.25 the trained rotations
recover part of the cost: the last-ring circuit beats the classical head on its products by
$6.4\pm5.8$ test points. The products thus reproduce the accuracy trend and most of the
trained ring circuits' readouts and gradient directions, and the trained rotations add task
detail on top of them.

\subsection{Without CNOTs the head is a classical sinusoidal model}
\label{sec:sinusoid}

\input{figures/table3_ringfree_classical}

By \cref{eq:ringfree}, the ring-free head computes
$W[\alpha_j\cos\enc{j}+\beta_j\sin\enc{j}]_j+b$ for every setting of its rotations.
\Cref{tab:ringfree} compares it with classical heads of this form trained under the same
protocol. The cos-only restriction trails it by $0.3\pm0.6$ probe and $0.6\pm0.7$ test points
with mixed signs, and the unrestricted cos+sin extension by 0.9 and 1.3 (3 seeds). The match
holds at the level of the function class, not the parameters: the trained ring-free circuit
has mean absolute $R_y$ angle 0.41 rad and uses the sine component (Jacobian
cosine 0.41 against the cos-only model; \cref{tab:zeroangle}).

\subsection{Controls that do not recover the gap}
\label{sec:controls}

\input{figures/table2_controls}

\Cref{tab:controls} tests alternative explanations on CIFAR-10/4,000. Lowering or raising the
backbone learning rate with the head learning rate fixed leaves a gap of 4.9--8.6 points at
every rate where the VQC trains, and the VQC collapses at the two highest rates. The
backbone's gradient norm is larger, not smaller, with the VQC head. Freezing the circuit's
rotations at initialization, which keeps the head close to its zero-rotation model, does
not recover the probe ($-1.6\pm0.7$ against the trained circuit). BatchNorm before the encoding
and two alternative encodings, one of which breaks the sign symmetry of
$\cos(\pi\tanh z)$, recover at most 0.7 probe points. Lowering product order gives by far the
largest recoveries.

Higher-order circuits also fit their training data less well under the 80-epoch budget
(clean training accuracy 96.6\% without CNOTs, 89.2\% for the default circuit and 65.6\% for a
ring after the last layer), so part of the gap could be under-training. Doubling the budget
to 160 epochs for every head, the linear one included, raises every probe but keeps the
ordering (\cref{tab:budget}). The default circuit then fits its training set nearly as well as
the ring-free and linear heads (98.8\% vs 99.9\%) and still probes $2.8\pm0.4$ points below
the ring-free head and $3.1\pm0.3$ below the linear one (5 seeds, every seed negative). The
last-ring circuit remains under-fit at 160 epochs (79.5\%), so its larger cost includes slower
optimization.

\subsection{A pretrained backbone and the effect of data size}
\label{sec:pretrained}

\input{figures/table4_pretrained}

Hybrid image classifiers are often built on a pretrained network rather than a small CNN
trained from scratch. We repeat the key heads on an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 fine-tuned
through each head, with its classifier replaced by the 8-dimensional bottleneck
(\cref{tab:pretrained}). The ordering carries over unchanged. At 4,000 images the ring-free
and pairs circuits probe within 0.2 points of the linear head, while the default circuit sits
$7.2\pm2.3$ points below it and the last-ring circuit $18.0\pm4.7$ below (5 seeds, every seed
negative); a two-layer MLP probe gives the same gaps to within a point. The cost depends
strongly on data: the default circuit trails by $22.3\pm2.9$ points at 1,000 images and by
$1.8\pm0.5$ at 10,000, while the ring-free head stays within 1.6 points of linear at every
size. Small quantum heads are usually proposed for exactly the low-data regime where this
cost is largest. One result differs from the small CNN: the classical head on the default
circuit's products costs less than the circuit itself ($-4.0\pm1.6$ vs $-7.2\pm2.3$; the
products probe $3.2\pm1.3$ points above the VQC, every seed positive), so on this backbone
the products reproduce the ordering but only part of the circuit's cost.
