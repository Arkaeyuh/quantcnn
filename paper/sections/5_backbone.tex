\section{What the VQC Head Does to the Backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}

\subsection{The default VQC head lowers bottleneck probe accuracy}
\label{sec:gap}

On CIFAR-10 with 4,000 images, the common probe reaches $69.8\pm1.0$ on backbones trained
with the default VQC head and $74.5\pm0.5$ on backbones trained with a linear head, a paired
difference of $-4.7\pm0.9$ points that is negative for all five seeds. The probe reads the
VQC-trained bottleneck better than the VQC head itself does ($67.8\pm1.3$ test): replacing
the VQC head by the probe raises accuracy by about 2.0 points, and a 4.7-point gap remains
between the two backbones under the common probe. The gap is larger with less data ($-11.2$ and $-11.1$ at 1,000 and
2,000 images) and smaller on MNIST ($-7.9$ to $-1.0$; 3 seeds each). A narrow classical head
lowers probe accuracy as well: MLP-8 backbones probe $3.6\pm0.6$ points below linear-head
backbones on CIFAR-10/4,000, $1.0\pm0.9$ above the VQC. The gap is not an artifact of linear
decoding: a two-layer MLP probe on the same bottlenecks recovers at most 0.4 points more than
the linear probe for any head, and puts the default VQC $4.8\pm0.9$ points below the linear
head (\cref{app:mechanism}), also when its learning-rate grid is extended downward. The gap
therefore persists under the tested linear and two-layer MLP probes; we do not claim more
about the information the bottleneck contains. The rest of this section shows that the VQC's share
of the gap depends on its CNOT structure, which no classical head has.

\subsection{Removing the CNOT rings recovers most of the gap}
\label{sec:ring}

Deleting every CNOT and keeping the rest of the head unchanged raises probe accuracy to
$73.6\pm0.5$ and test accuracy to $73.1\pm0.5$ (5 seeds). Against the default VQC this is
$+3.8\pm1.2$ probe and $+5.2\pm1.4$ test points, positive for every seed, and the ring-free
head now probes $2.8\pm0.6$ points above MLP-8. It leaves $0.8\pm0.5$ probe and $1.4\pm0.5$
test points to the linear head. On MNIST, ring removal gains 2.8 and 1.4 probe
points at 500 and 1,000 images.

\subsection{Product order tracks the gap}
\label{sec:order}

Which property of the rings matters? \Cref{fig:hero}b places every head at the product order
of \cref{eq:identity}. Across the eleven ring-family heads, at four orders, every head's mean
probe accuracy is below that of every lower-order head. Depth and parameter count do not explain
this: a ring after the last layer keeps the default's parameter count and rotation layers but
raises the order to 5.25 and lowers the probe by $9.7\pm2.2$ points (5 seeds), and the 1- and
2-layer circuits at that order probe at 56.8 and 61.2 (3 seeds).

Order also carries over to layouts it was not fit on. For six CNOT layouts outside the ring
family, chosen by order alone, we predicted probe accuracy from the ring-family fit before
training. The association holds (Spearman $-0.89$, against a threshold of $-0.8$ set in advance;
five of six within 4 points), with two rank reversals. The largest miss, brickwork at 5 points
above its prediction, is the layout trained furthest from its product model. Order extends to any
rotations: expanding each readout operator $U(\theta)^\dagger Z_kU(\theta)=\sum_Pc_PP$ in Pauli strings,
the \emph{effective order} is the $c_P^2$-weighted mean number of inputs touched by the strings visible on
the encoded state, which equals $\order$ at $\theta=0$ (\cref{app:efforder}). At its trained rotations
brickwork's effective order is 2.6 rather than 3.5, below the distance-2 ring's 3.0 (\cref{fig:hero}b,
\cref{app:family}). Under $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tanh z+\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ six layouts follow order
as predicted ($-0.94$); under a phase encoding, whose zero-rotation readouts vanish, probe accuracy
follows the circuits' $R_y$ order ($-0.99$, \cref{app:encoding,app:phase}). At 12 qubits the
ordering holds as predicted, with the default ring again 4.0 points below the ring-free one
(\cref{app:width}). Order is a useful
but coarse descriptor.

Two interventions test the products directly. First, classical heads trained end to end on a
circuit's zero-rotation products, with no circuit, land at the circuit's order on the trend:
the products of the default circuit give $68.8\pm1.6$ probe and $67.1\pm1.3$ test, paired
differences to the default VQC of $-1.0\pm2.2$ and $-0.7\pm1.7$ with mixed signs across
seeds. Summing instead of multiplying the same cosines would give the order-1 cos head's function
class (73.3 probe), as every CNOT-derived support matrix has full rank, though such a head would
train differently. Second, the
\emph{pairs} circuit keeps a CNOT after every layer but has order 1, because its pair CNOTs
cancel at zero rotations. It recovers $3.1\pm1.2$ probe and $4.4\pm1.2$ test points over the
default, positive for every seed, and sits $0.7\pm1.0$ probe points below the ring-free
circuit. Pairs after the
last layer only (order 1.5) probe at $71.2\pm0.7$, between orders 1 and 2.5, as does the
classical head on their products ($71.2\pm1.3$).

\input{figures/tableM_generality}

Order needs neither CNOTs, entanglement nor a saturating encoding (\cref{tab:generality}). A CZ ring entangles, but CZ commutes with $Z$, so its order is 1: it probes $3.4\pm0.8$ points above the default CNOT ring (every seed) and
$0.4\pm0.8$ below the ring-free head. Conversely, two- and three-qubit $Z$ readouts of the
ring-free circuit, which has no entangling gate, probe at 70.3 and 67.4, against 70.4 and 67.3 from
the ring-family fit. Under the unbounded encoding $\enc{j}=z_j$ four layouts keep their order ranking.

In CNOT-derived supports, order rises with overlap, the number of inputs two readouts share.
Classical product heads on designed supports separate the two: at equal overlap and full rank an
order-3 star probes $5.4\pm2.7$ points above an order-4 layout (5 seeds, all positive), and at equal
order how the coupling is distributed also matters (\cref{tab:support}). Order tracks lower mean
probe accuracy but does not determine it.

The pattern replicates on MNIST with 1,000 images under an ordering logged before the runs but committed after them (\cref{app:repro}): probe
accuracy is 96.5, 95.5, 95.1 and 91.6 at orders 1, 1.5, 2.5 and 5.25 (\cref{app:support}).

The identity is guaranteed at zero rotations and generally fails at trained ones. An affine map from each trained circuit's zero-rotation
readouts to its trained readouts recovers 87\% and 90\% of the held-out readout variance for the
default and last-ring circuits (\cref{app:zeroangle}), and the cost does not depend on starting in the
product regime: with rotations initialized in $\mathcal{U}(-\pi,\pi)$ the default circuit still probes
$4.6\pm1.7$ points below the ring-free one (\cref{tab:init}).

\subsection{Without CNOTs the head is a classical sinusoidal model}
\label{sec:sinusoid}

\input{figures/table3_ringfree_classical}

By \cref{eq:ringfree}, the ring-free head computes
$W[\alpha_j\cos\enc{j}+\beta_j\sin\enc{j}]_j+b$ for every setting of its rotations.
\Cref{tab:ringfree} compares it with classical heads of this form trained under the same
protocol. The cos-only restriction trails it by $0.3\pm0.6$ probe and $0.6\pm0.7$ test points
with mixed signs, and the unrestricted cos+sin extension by 0.9 and 1.3 (3 seeds). The match
holds at the level of the function class, not the parameters: the trained ring-free circuit
has mean absolute $R_y$ angle 0.41 rad and uses the sine component (logit-Jacobian
cosine 0.41 against the cos-only model, \cref{app:zeroangle}).

\subsection{Controls that do not recover the gap}
\label{sec:controls}

\input{figures/table4_pretrained}

\Cref{tab:controls} (\cref{app:controls}) tests alternative explanations on CIFAR-10/4,000. Lowering or raising the
backbone learning rate with the head learning rate fixed leaves a gap of 4.9--8.6 points at
every rate where the VQC trains, and the VQC collapses at the two highest rates. The
backbone's gradient norm is larger, not smaller, with the VQC head. Freezing the circuit's
rotations at initialization, which keeps the head close to its zero-rotation model, does
not recover the probe ($-1.6\pm0.7$ against the trained circuit). BatchNorm before the encoding
and two alternative encodings, one of which breaks the sign symmetry of
$\cos(\pi\tanh z)$, recover at most 0.7 probe points. Lowering product order recovers by far the most.

Higher-order circuits also fit their training data less well under the 80-epoch budget
(clean training accuracy 96.6\% without CNOTs, 89.2\% for the default circuit and 65.6\% for a
ring after the last layer), so part of the gap could be under-training. Doubling the budget
to 160 epochs for the linear, default, ring-free and last-ring heads (CIFAR-10/4,000)
raises every probe but keeps the ordering (\cref{tab:budget}). The default circuit then fits its training set nearly as well as
the ring-free and linear heads (98.8\% vs 99.9\%) and still probes $2.8\pm0.4$ points below
the ring-free head and $3.1\pm0.3$ below the linear one (5 seeds, every seed negative). The
last-ring circuit remains under-fit at 160 epochs (79.5\%), so its larger cost includes slower
optimization.

\subsection{A pretrained backbone, data size and training budget}
\label{sec:pretrained}


Hybrid image classifiers are often built on a pretrained network. We repeat the key heads on
an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 fine-tuned through each head, its classifier replaced by the
8-dimensional bottleneck (\cref{tab:pretrained}). The ordering carries over: at 4,000 images
the ring-free and pairs circuits probe within 0.2 points of the linear head, the default
circuit $7.2\pm2.3$ below it and the last-ring circuit $18.0\pm4.7$ below (5 seeds, every seed
negative); on the three seeds probed with both, an MLP probe gives the same default gap
($-5.8$ vs $-5.7$). At a fixed 30
epochs the cost falls with training size, from $22.3\pm2.9$ points at 1,000 images to
$1.9\pm0.4$ at 10,000, but smaller sets then also get fewer optimizer updates. At an
approximately matched number of updates (about 3,800) the default circuit costs $6.6\pm1.7$,
$5.7\pm0.7$ and $7.2\pm2.1$ points at 1,000, 4,000 and 10,000 images, and the ring-free head
stays within 0.5 points of linear (seeds 0--2; \cref{tab:ptbudget}); re-selecting the learning
rates gives similar costs. Training budget therefore
accounts for much of the size dependence, though the two are not fully separated.

High-order heads are unstable on this backbone (\cref{app:ptcontrols}).

\subsection{Why order costs: a signal budget and a saturation trap}
\label{sec:saturation}

\input{figures/tableM_saturation}

Differentiating \cref{eq:identity} at zero rotations gives, for $j\in\Sk$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \ZZ{k}}{\partial z_j}
  = -\pi\,\mathrm{sech}^2(z_j)\,\sin\enc{j}\prod_{i\in\Sk\setminus\{j\}}\cos\enc{i},
  \label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
so a coordinate's gradient is scaled by the cosines of the other coordinates in $\Sk$
(\cref{prop:atten}) and by its own $\mathrm{sech}^2(z_j)$. A readout also keeps a useful
magnitude only if its factors stay near $\pm1$, a signal budget:
\begin{proposition}[Signal budget]\label{prop:budget}
Let $r_k=\prod_{j\in\Sk}\cos\enc{j}$ with $|\Sk|=m$, $\enc{j}=\pi\tanh z_j$ and independent factors, and
let $\mathbb{E}|r_k|\ge a>0$. Then some $j\in\Sk$ has
$\Pr\big(\delta<|\tanh z_j|<1-\delta\big)\le(1-a^{1/m})/(1-\cos\pi\delta)$ for every
$\delta\in(0,\tfrac12)$; if the factors are identically distributed, every $j\in\Sk$ does. Without
independence, every $j\in\Sk$ satisfies the bound with $a$ in place of $a^{1/m}$.
\end{proposition}
As $m$ grows, $a^{1/m}\to1$, so high-order inputs gather at $z\approx0$ or at
saturation. On the trained
bottlenecks the middle-zone fraction ($0.1\le|\tanh z|\le0.9$) is 0.84, 0.63 and 0.46 at orders 1, 2.5
and 5.25, and the lost mass goes to saturation (30--49\% of coordinates for the two ring circuits, 1\%
without CNOTs), which tracks probe accuracy across the 28 VQC configurations ($\rho=-0.91$; \cref{tab:geometry}).

Both ends of the encoding are flat (\cref{prop:flat}): with $t=|\tanh z|$,
$|\tfrac{d}{dz}\cos(\pi\tanh z)|\le\pi^2t$ near zero and $\le8\pi^2e^{-4|z|}$ at saturation, the square of $\tanh$'s
decay, because $\sin\enc{j}$ vanishes there too. Under \cref{prop:budget} with fixed $a$ and $\delta$, the probability that an
input's sensitivity exceeds $\pi^2\delta$ vanishes as order grows. Every
head saturates early in training ($S=0.44$--$0.77$ at epoch 5, 3 seeds); the ring-free head then
desaturates to 0.02, while the default and last-ring heads stall at 0.30 and 0.46 and their probe gaps
peak at epochs 15 and 35 (\cref{app:dynamics}). We tested this trap with a pre-registered intervention
that leaves the forward pass unchanged: our escape-only variant of the straight-through estimator \citep{bengio2013}, which in the backward
pass drops $\mathrm{sech}^2(z_j)$ when the descent step moves $z_j$ toward 0, so saturated
coordinates can leave (a plain version made every circuit diverge, and we redesigned it after; \cref{app:dynamics}). It removes saturation in every head (mean $S\le0.005$) and raises
probe accuracy by $6.8\pm2.2$ points on the last-ring circuit and $1.5\pm0.9$ on the default, positive
in every seed, against $0.9\pm0.5$ without CNOTs (\cref{tab:satmain}). The default gain misses the committed $+1.5$ by 0.02; a pre-registered
replication on seeds 5--9 meets every criterion ($+3.3$, $+5.6$ and $+1.3$; \cref{app:dynamics}). A penalty against saturation, which also changes the loss, gives similar
gains (\cref{app:saturation}).

With saturation removed, order still costs: the escape-only circuits probe at 74.5, 71.3 and 66.9 at
orders 1, 2.5 and 5.25. Releasing the trap recovers half of the last-ring cost and 1.5 of the default's 3.8 points (44\% and 15\% if both arms are treated); the rest is consistent with the budget, though not uniquely attributed to it.
